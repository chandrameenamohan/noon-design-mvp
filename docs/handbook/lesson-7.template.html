Multi-node: the lease, the clock, and the fence

The win: you can explain, to an interviewer or to yourself, why a lease is a lock with a time on it and why that time may only be read from your own monotonic clock counting from the moment you sent; why a Lua script is the unit of atomicity in Redis and what breaks when the token is issued outside it; why heartbeats may route a peer but never decide who owns a room, and why a dead owner's lease is waited out rather than stolen; why a process paused past its lease cannot be stopped by any check it performs itself, and what Kleppmann's fencing token is; why the fence must sit in the same SQL statement as the write, and what FOR UPDATE does there; where this design sits against ZooKeeper, etcd and Redisson, and what it gives up under CAP; and you can point at the line of code for every claim.

D3 · Explain a decision before reading mine. Answer out loud or on paper, then open the answer.

1. A holder could trust its lease until "the renewal's reply arrived plus ttl", read from Date.now(). Instead it trusts until "the renewal was sent plus 0.9 ttl" on performance.now(), and the doc comment says this is "not safety". Defend all three choices, then say what the fourth mechanism is that makes it safe.

Redis starts its expiry countdown when the command arrives, which is after we sent it; so a deadline counted from the send can never outlast Redis's, while one counted from the reply trusts the lease for a whole round trip longer than Redis will keep it, and under a slow network that is a window in which two nodes both believe they own the room. The 0.9 is the allowance for the two clocks running at slightly different rates. performance.now() is monotonic: it cannot jump when NTP corrects the wall clock, and a backwards jump of Date.now() would extend a deadline by exactly the jump. "Not safety" because none of this runs while the process is frozen: a pause of two seconds inside an await resumes with the flag still true and the next instruction already chosen, and the learning test measured the other node owning the room 1.7 s before the frozen one could notice. The fourth mechanism is the fence: the storage compares a token the writer received when it became the owner, in the statement that writes, so what a frozen process believes is irrelevant to what lands. The clock bounds failover time; the token gives safety; neither does the other's job.

2. When an owner's heartbeat stops, the other node could take the room at once. Instead it sends new peers to a live node that polls until the dead lease expires, up to about one ttl, and never steals. Why, and why is the same rule applied to a node's own previous lease?

Because "stopped beating" is not "stopped writing". A killed node does neither; a paused node does neither and then wakes; a node cut off from Redis stops beating and keeps its Postgres connection. Stealing on a missed heartbeat would make the paused case the normal one: two nodes owning a room from the moment of the steal until the old one's clock runs out, and every op the old one accepts in that window an op its peers were told about that the fence must then refuse. Waiting for the expiry keeps the invariant that at most one node believes it owns the room at any moment, pauses aside, and turns the fence into the rare backstop it should be. Liveness therefore only chooses where a peer is sent (a wrong guess costs one 4409), and the lease alone says who owns. A node's own dead lease is the same case seen from inside: the previous run was killed mid-room, its lease is real until Redis lets it go, and the new run cannot renew a holder value it does not have, so the honest thing is to wait exactly as it would for a stranger's. Doing that on the server, with the peer held connecting, is also what bounds failover to one ttl rather than a ttl plus a client backoff.

3. The fence compares a random claim id in insert ... select ... where fence_claim is not distinct from $9 for update, and claim uses strictly <. Why not read the claim first in TypeScript and then insert; why a claim id and not "token at least the largest seen"; why < and not <=; and what does for update buy?

Read-then-write has an await between the two, and a process can be frozen for a minute inside an await: A reads its claim (valid), freezes, B claims and reads the journal, A wakes and inserts a row one past what B read. The naive control in fence.int performs exactly that and asserts the zombie row is in the journal. In one statement the check and the write cannot be separated by a pause. A token high-water mark fails for two reasons specific to this repo: the counter lives in Redis and a flush restarts it, so the floor from fence_token is needed to keep tokens rising at all; and even then one token can be issued twice (a node paused between acquire and claim, then a flush that reissues the number), so the thing an append carries is an id of this one opening, and the loser of two openings under an equal token cannot write. < rather than <= is the same case at the claim: with <= both openings under token 7 would claim and both would hold a valid claim id. for update takes a row lock on the documents row so that an append and a claim serialise: the claim waits for an in-flight insert to commit (and the new owner's replay then contains the row), or the claim commits first and the blocked append re-evaluates its where against the updated row and inserts nothing. Without the lock the two could interleave at the statement level and the "waits, then is refused" test would not hold.

Recall first

Look away. Name the three Lua scripts and what each one checks before it acts. Then name the four things a node does when its lease is lost or its append is fenced. Then check.

Check yourself

ACQUIRE checks "nobody holds it" and then lifts the counter to the floor, INCRs a token and SETs with PX, all in one script; RENEW checks "the value is still exactly mine" and PEXPIREs; RELEASE checks the same and DELs. On a lost lease or a Fenced append, drop: marks the room lost, stops its snapshots, forgets the room (so the next peer asks Redis again), and closes every socket with 4409, which peer-client treats as "ask /session again".

Then test it

Eight questions. Options are shuffled each visit.

1. Ten peers of a new document join at once, five to each node. What token does the winner hold, and why does that number mean anything?

The join-race test asserts token: 1 after the race and 2 after the room closes and is taken again.

2. A renewal is sent at 300 ms and answered at 1100 ms (ttl 1000). Until when does the holder trust the lease?

Counting from the reply would trust the lease for a whole round trip longer than Redis keeps it. The test with the slow renewal is the mutation check.

3. The owner of a room stops beating. What does the other node do when a peer of that room arrives?

"Stopped beating" is not "stopped writing" (a paused node wakes). Liveness routes; the lease alone owns.

4. Why does a room that is fenced drop (4409 to every peer) rather than go read-only and heal, as it does when Postgres is down?

watchFence tells the server once; onReadOnly checks fenced so the heal loop never starts.

5. Why is the fence check inside the insert ... select ... where fence_claim is not distinct from $9 for update statement rather than a select in TypeScript before the insert?

The naive control in fence.int performs the interleaving and asserts the zombie's row IS in the journal. Drill 2 plants it.

6. Redis is flushed while both nodes run. Why do tokens keep rising, and why is that not enough on its own?

The lease integration test deletes the counter mid-test and asserts 43 follows 42; fence.int asserts a second claim under the same token is refused.

7. What does for update add to the append statement?

The test "an append racing an uncommitted claim waits for it, then is refused" holds a second connection's transaction open for 300 ms and asserts the append has not settled.

8. What does this design give up, in CAP terms, and why is that the right side?

LEASE_TTL_MS is the dial; the chaos run's budget is expressed in ttl so the test moves with it.
