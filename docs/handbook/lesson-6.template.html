D3 · Explain a decision before reading mine. Answer out loud or on paper, then open the answer.

1. The room could apply and broadcast an op, then journal it, and refuse it if the write fails. That is faster for the sender. Why is the acknowledgement given only after the append, and why is the sequence number taken only then?

Because an acknowledgement is a promise about the future: "after a crash this will still be true". Only the database can make that promise, so the room may only relay it after the database has spoken. Announce first and three things break at once. Every other peer applies an op that may not exist after a restart, so two tabs diverge from the truth. The sender's client marks the op confirmed and forgets it, so nothing will ever resend it: the op is lost while everyone saw it. And the room's seq has moved for a row that is not in the journal, so the next append's number is one ahead of the table, which is the room's own signal for a rival writer; under the reversed order it becomes a permanent read-only after the first failed write. Taking the number inside accept, after the append resolved, means a failed write leaves no gap and the next op simply gets the same number. The cost is one database round trip per op inside the room's queue, which is the price of the promise. Drill 2 plants the reverse order; its first assertion is peer B having received seq 2.

2. When the journal fails, the room could ping Postgres every second and say "writable" when it answers. Instead recover() reads since(seq) and replays it first. Why, and what exactly does "no acknowledged op is lost, none is applied twice" require of the client and the journal together?

Because "the database answers" and "the room agrees with the database" are different facts. A rejected append may have landed (the connection died after the commit) and a timed-out one may land later; a rival row may sit at the room's next seq. If the room goes writable without reading, the landed op is in the journal and in nobody's document, and the next append from anyone breaks op_journal_seq and puts the room back into read-only for ever. Replaying through accept applies and announces what landed, moves the room's seq past it, and only then says writable; the sender's held resend is then answered from memory with the seq it got. The promise needs three things. From the journal: a unique key on (document, sender, opId), and the original row returned when it is broken, so a second sending gets the first answer. From the client: keep the op under the same opId until it is answered, and re-offer it after every welcome and every "writable", in order. From the room: acknowledge only what is durable, refuse everything while it cannot write, and read the journal before it writes again. Remove any one and the promise is gone: no key, and a resend is applied twice; no held resend, and an in-flight op is lost; no replay, and a landed op is heard by nobody.

3. Snapshots are written to MinIO with IfNoneMatch: "*", and documents.snapshot_seq is updated only afterwards and only forwards. A failed snapshot is logged and never retried, and F8's idle save was deleted. Defend each of those four choices in one sentence, then say what a snapshot is.

A snapshot is a materialised prefix of the log, so everything follows from the journal being the truth. Conditional put: two rooms (or one room twice, after a crash) may reach the same seq, and the same journal prefix gives the same document, so the loser of the race loses nothing and a plain overwrite would only open a window where the object is half-written. Pointer after the object, forwards only: a crash between the two writes leaves an orphaned object (harmless) rather than a pointer to nothing (a document that cannot open), and a slow write of an older snapshot finishing late must not move the document backwards. Never retried: a snapshot that failed to write costs nothing but more rows to replay at the next open, so a retry loop would be complexity spent protecting a copy. F8 deleted: F8 had to retry for ever because the room's memory was the only copy of the edits since the last save; with the journal that is never true, and keeping two save paths means two truths. The key is zero-padded because S3 sorts strings, and the newest snapshot is found by the pointer rather than by listing because S3 lists ascending in pages of 1000, measured. What it is: a cache of the log that may be lost but must never be wrong, which is why the document is encoded in the same tick as its seq.

Check yourself

After the append resolves, accept: applies the op to the in-memory document, takes the sequence number (only now), remembers the op under sender:opId, and broadcasts it to every peer; the sender's own copy of that broadcast is its acknowledgement. Two starting points: the newest snapshot in MinIO (named by documents.snapshot_seq), or what F8's idle save wrote before E6.2 if that is newer; after either, since(seq) replays only the journal rows past that seq, and the room starts at the last replayed seq.

Then test it

Eight questions. Options are shuffled each visit.

1. The room builds sequenced with seq + 1 before the append. When is the number actually taken, and why does it matter?

Under the reversed order a failed write would move the room one ahead of the table, and the next append would break op_journal_seq.

2. Both unique constraints fail with SQLSTATE 23505. How does append tell a resend from a rival writer, and what does it do for each?

Measured in learning-tests/postgres: the driver exposes err.constraint. "constraint" in err narrows the type so the compare compiles without a cast.

3. Why is the journal's dedupe key (document, actor, opId) and not (document, opId)?

The database test proves two senders may share an opId and both rows are written.

4. A journal append fails. In what order do peers hear about it, and what does a read-only room acknowledge?

Even an answer from memory is an acknowledgement. The order lets the sender's client treat the refusal as "hold", not "resync".

5. What does recover() do before it tells peers "writable", and why is a bare health probe wrong?

"The database answers" and "the room agrees with the database" are different facts. The test with committedAnyway is the lost-reply case.

6. Why is the snapshot written to MinIO before documents.snapshot_seq is updated, and what does where snapshot_seq < $3 add?

A 412 on the put is treated as success: the same journal prefix gives the same document.

7. A snapshot fails to write. What happens?

F8 had to retry for ever because the room's memory was the only copy. That is no longer true, so its retry loop was deleted with it.
